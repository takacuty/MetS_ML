
Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a major risk factor for 
cardiovascular disease (CVD) and type 2 diabetes mellitus. As populations age worldwide, extending healthy life expectancy has become an urgent priority. Japan, a super-aged society, introduced the national “Specific Health Checkups (SHC) and Specific Health Guidance” program in 2008 to detect and address MetS early. However, MetS prevalence continues to rise more than a decade after the program began. The current system may contribute to this trend by classifying individuals as high risk only when they have abdominal obesity. Yet multiple metabolic abnormalities can confer elevated cardiovascular risk among individuals who do not meet this criterion. Predictive models based on health checkup data are therefore needed to forecast disease onset accurately and enable preemptive interventions for high-risk individuals. 

Predictive models for MetS onset are being actively developed worldwide. Risk scores based on SHC data have also been reported in Japan. Recent studies have applied machine learning to disease risk prediction. Despite these advances, existing studies of MetS have largely focused on diagnosing current MetS or predicting its onset within one to two years. Because MetS develops after metabolic abnormalities accumulate over many years, early assessment of future onset risk is important. Incident MetS cases are often underrepresented in health checkup datasets, creating class imbalance and hindering accurate detection. Resampling and threshold adjustment have been proposed to address this imbalance, but their effectiveness for long-term MetS prediction remains unclear. The primary challenge is therefore to identify an optimal modeling method that accurately predicts MetS onset.

Earlier MetS prediction models often limited the number of predictors to reduce overfitting and improve interpretability. Studies by the authors have shown that MetS risk is influenced by diverse factors, including standard blood markers and lifestyle factors. Moreover, multiple risk factors can interact synergistically to increase MetS risk. Excessive variable restriction may therefore overlook important predictors and interaction effects, reducing predictive accuracy. Recent predictive modeling studies recommend leveraging all available data and evaluating internal validity during model development. A second challenge is therefore to improve predictive accuracy while maintaining clinically acceptable interpretability.

Against this background, this study aims to develop a model that predicts MetS onset within five years using a large-scale health checkup dataset and to evaluate its predictive performance and interpretability. The study addresses three key aspects. First, it targets medium- to long-term prediction with high clinical relevance, enabling preventive interventions before disease onset—an opportunity unavailable with short-term models. Second, it compares learning strategies for imbalanced data to identify the strategy that yields the most reliable screening performance. Third, it uses all available health checkup variables without prior selection and applies SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to interpret model predictions. This approach captures nonlinear relationships and interactions among risk factors while enabling interpretation of individual risk profiles. Beyond improving predictive accuracy, the framework offers a novel approach to integrating large-scale health checkup data into clinical practice and public health policy.

Methods

Data Source
This study used 12 years of SHC data (fiscal years 2011–2022) obtained from the Kokuho Database (KDB), which is managed by the Gunma Association of National Health Insurance Organizations. The KDB is a public administrative database that integrates local residents’ health checkup records and medical claims data, covering approximately 40% of insured residents in Gunma Prefecture.

Study Population
Figure 1 shows the participant selection process. The initial population comprised 1,069,543 National Health Insurance beneficiary records from Gunma Prefecture for individuals who underwent SHC during fiscal years 2011–2017 (2011: 150,610; 2012: 154,579; 2013: 156,454; 2014: 156,531; 2015: 155,690; 2016: 151,068; 2017: 144,611). Incident MetS was assessed using health checkup data collected five years after baseline. The following exclusion criteria were applied: 594,166 records without five-year follow-up data; 326,513 duplicate records, with only the most recent visit retained for participants with multiple records; and 294 records with missing data that precluded MetS assessment. In addition, 11,259 individuals with a history of stroke, heart disease, or chronic renal failure recorded in the standard questionnaire and 18,299 individuals who met the MetS criteria at baseline were excluded. These criteria yielded a final analytical population of 119,012 participants. Baseline characteristics were also compared between the analytical cohort and the excluded group to assess potential selection bias.

Data Cleaning
This analysis included the following variables from the Specific Health Checkup data: sex and age at the end of each fiscal year, calculated from the date of birth and examination date, as demographic characteristics; body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) as anthropometric measures; and systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GTP), and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) as laboratory values. Eleven lifestyle questions with responses available across multiple years were also included (Supplementary Table 1), which defines risk status for each behavior. For alcohol consumption, participants who answered “every day” or “sometimes” to Question 9 (drinking frequency) and “1 go (approximately 180 mL) or more” to Question 10 (amount) were classified as “risk present.” Those who answered “rarely” to Question 9 or “less than 1 go” to Question 10 were classified as “risk absent.” Self-administered questionnaires also collected information on medication use for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, as well as histories of stroke, heart disease, and kidney disease.
Variables with missing rates of 20% or higher were excluded. For categorical variables with missing rates of 5% to less than 20%, missingness indicators were created. For variables with missing rates below 5%, continuous variables were median-imputed and categorical variables were mode-imputed.

Outcome Definition
According to the criteria of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, MetS was defined as a waist circumference of ≥85 cm in men or ≥90 cm in women, plus at least two of the following three components: (1) systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mmHg, or antihypertensive medication use; (2) HbA1c ≥6.0% or glucose-lowering medication use; and (3) HDL-C <40 mg/dL or lipid-lowering medication use.

Machine Learning Model Construction
This study used LightGBM as the predictive model. LightGBM is a gradient-boosting decision tree (GBDT) framework that enables rapid training on large, high-dimensional datasets through Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB). Previous medical studies have demonstrated its strong predictive performance on large health checkup datasets. Optuna, an automated hyperparameter optimization framework, was used to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) on the validation set. The tuned parameters included the number of leaves (num_leaves), learning rate (learning_rate), minimum number of data points per leaf (min_data_in_leaf), maximum number of bins (max_bin), minimum split gain (min_gain_to_split), and L1/L2 regularization coefficients (lambda_l1 and lambda_l2). Early stopping terminated training when validation log loss failed to improve for 100 consecutive iterations, thereby helping prevent overfitting.
To address class imbalance in the health checkup data, two modeling strategies were used:
Full-data model: The model was trained on the complete training dataset to maximize overall discrimination, as measured by AUC.
Under-bagging model: This ensemble approach combined undersampling and bagging. At each iteration, all minority-class observations (incident cases) were combined with an equal number of majority-class observations (non-cases) randomly sampled from the training data. After 50 iterations, the final predicted probability was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the probabilities predicted by the 50 models.

Statistical Analysis and Performance Evaluation
Model performance was evaluated using stratified five-fold cross-validation. The dataset was divided into five folds while preserving the class distribution of the outcome. In each iteration, four folds were used for model development and the remaining fold for evaluation. Each development set was further divided into training and validation sets for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping. Repeating this procedure across all five folds generated out-of-fold predicted probabilities for every participant.
Predictive performance was assessed using several metrics. Discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using the Brier score and reliability diagrams: the Brier score quantified overall calibration error, whereas the reliability diagrams compared predicted probabilities with observed event rates. Model-specific decision thresholds were then used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score as follows:
Full-data model: The optimal threshold was selected using the Youden index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity on the training ROC curve.
Under-bagging model: Because undersampling produced a 1:1 class ratio, the threshold was fixed at 0.5, corresponding to equal predicted probabilities for the two classes. For Brier score calculation and reliability diagrams, predicted probabilities were adjusted for the sampling-induced shift in class prevalence using the actual prevalence. 
Sex-stratified subgroup analyses assessed differences in risk factors and predictive accuracy between sexes. SHAP values were used to quantify feature importance and assess model interpretability. SHAP values calculated from the validation dataset in each fold were concatenated across all five folds to assess global feature importance. For the Under-bagging Model, SHAP values were averaged across all base learners within each fold. All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.10) using a two-sided significance level of 5%.

Research Ethics
This study was a secondary analysis of existing KDB data. Because the data had already been anonymized at the time of acquisition, informed consent was not obtained. The study protocol and waiver of informed consent were approved by the Gunma University Ethics Committee for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (approval no. HS2023-137) in accordance with the “Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects” issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Participants
The analytical population comprised 119,012 participants. At the 5-year follow-up, 10,633 participants (6,144 men and 4,489 women) developed MetS, corresponding to an incidence rate of 8.9%. Table 1 compares the baseline characteristics of participants who did and did not develop MetS. The incident MetS group was significantly older and had higher BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, TG, LDL-C, AST, ALT, γ-GTP, and HbA1c levels, but lower HDL-C levels (all p < 0.001). Additionally, the incident MetS group had significantly higher proportions of participants using medications for hypertension, dyslipidemia, or diabetes and reporting unhealthy lifestyle behaviors in several domains, including smoking, weight gain since age 20, physical activity, walking speed, eating speed, eating dinner shortly before bedtime, skipping breakfast, and alcohol consumption. In contrast, the proportion of participants with risky sleep habits was significantly lower in the incident MetS group.

Model Performance
Table 2 and Figure 2 (ROC curves) summarize the predictive performance of each model on the validation data. In five-fold cross-validation, the Full-data Model achieved an AUC of 0.8692 ± 0.004. The optimal threshold was 0.0864 (range across folds, 0.0825–0.0902), yielding a sensitivity of 0.8454 ± 0.010, specificity of 0.7466 ± 0.009, and F1 score of 0.3820 ± 0.006. The Under-bagging Model achieved an AUC of 0.8682 ± 0.005. Using a fixed threshold of 0.5 yielded a sensitivity of 0.8458 ± 0.007, specificity of 0.7416 ± 0.004, and F1 score of 0.3774 ± 0.005.
The Full-data Model achieved a Brier score of 0.066 ± 0.0001, with close agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates on the reliability diagram (Figure 3). The Under-bagging Model yielded a Brier score of 0.065 ± 0.0001 (Figure 4).

Sex-Stratified Subgroup Analysis
Table 3 presents the results of the sex-stratified subgroup analysis. In five-fold cross-validation, the Full-data Model achieved an AUC of 0.8222 ± 0.003, sensitivity of 0.8248 ± 0.021, specificity of 0.6786 ± 0.030, and F1 score of 0.4248 ± 0.012 among men. Among women, it achieved an AUC of 0.8864 ± 0.004, sensitivity of 0.8370 ± 0.034, specificity of 0.7844 ± 0.031, and F1 score of 0.3270 ± 0.021.
The Under-bagging Model achieved an AUC of 0.8198 ± 0.003, sensitivity of 0.8096 ± 0.009, specificity of 0.6884 ± 0.007, and F1 score of 0.4248 ± 0.004 among men. Among women, it achieved an AUC of 0.8850 ± 0.004, sensitivity of 0.8534 ± 0.013, specificity of 0.7662 ± 0.007, and F1 score of 0.3134 ± 0.006.

Feature Importance
Figure 5 shows global feature importance based on SHAP values. In the Full-data Model, waist circumference was the most important predictor of MetS, followed by BMI, sex, and HbA1c. Sex-stratified analyses identified distinct predictor profiles: waist circumference, HbA1c, and HDL-C were the top predictors in men, whereas waist circumference, BMI, and lipid-lowering medication use ranked highest in women.
In the Under-bagging Model, waist circumference was also the most important predictor overall, followed by HbA1c, age, and BMI. The sex-stratified results were consistent with those of the Full-data Model: waist circumference, HbA1c, and HDL-C were the top predictors in men, whereas waist circumference, BMI, and lipid-lowering medication use ranked highest in women. These consistent patterns indicate that key predictors differed by sex regardless of the modeling approach.
